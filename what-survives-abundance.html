What Survives Abundance

October 2026 · Draft

A working draft. I'll come back to this one.

After writing about Palantir and Figma, I noticed I kept arriving at the same picture from different directions. When a tool makes building things easier, an industry doesn't just get more efficient. It reorganizes. The layer whose value came from the difficulty of making gets squeezed, and value moves to three places: whoever provides the capability, whoever decides what to make, and whoever controls where it gets seen. With AI, that means model providers, the people using models, and distributors.

This isn't new. The printing press ended the scribes and enriched printers and booksellers. Power looms ended the handloom weavers and enriched factory owners and merchants. What's different with AI is breadth. Those revolutions hit one craft at a time, over decades. AI is hitting design, software and video at once, which is why the same shape showed up in three companies I picked for unrelated reasons.

Netflix is the most interesting of the three, because it sits on two layers at once.

For most of its life, Netflix's edge was structural, not creative. It started as a distributor, licensing other studios' libraries until "Netflix and chill" meant staying in and streaming anything at all. Then it became a producer. It spent $100 million on two seasons of House of Cards, and releasing it all at once turned binge-watching into a habit. Its real advantage was scale: a show costs the same whether one million or three hundred million people watch it, and Netflix had the biggest paying audience, so it could outspend everyone and still make money. That's how it beat the studios. Not by entering their industry on their terms, but by coming at it from a different direction until their advantages stopped mattering.

That story should make Netflix nervous, because it's now on the other side of it.

The producer half of Netflix is exposed to the same force that hit Figma. Netflix already uses generative AI in roughly 300 titles this year, mostly to make production faster and cheaper. That's a saving, but it also erodes a moat built on affording what others couldn't. When production gets cheap, outspending everyone stops being a barrier.

The distributor half is contested by YouTube, which plays a different game entirely. Netflix pays for everything it shows. YouTube's creators fund their own work, and AI makes every one of them more capable. In July, YouTube reached a record 14.2% of US TV viewing, while Netflix's share slipped to a multi-year low. The comparison isn't perfect. In my experience YouTube is mostly something you watch alone and in short bursts, while Netflix is shows, often watched with someone. But Nielsen only counts TV screens, the living-room setting Netflix used to own. Different products can still compete for the same evening.

So what's left? History offers a better answer than the spreadsheets. When film arrived, people didn't stop going to plays. Theater shrank from mass entertainment into something smaller, but it survived, because it sells what a recording can't: being there, a performance that happens once, a room full of people reacting together. Chess is the same. Computers have beaten the best humans since 1997, and people watch human chess more than ever. When making becomes abundant, what survives is what's live, shared and human. Not as a mass market, but as culture.

Netflix seems to sense this. Its biggest recent bets are NFL games, live events, and shows big enough that everyone watches them the same week. Stranger Things and a Christmas Day of football are cultural moments in a way a feed can't easily be, because YouTube's strength is that everyone watches something different.

But becoming culture is harder than buying live sports. Theater is cultural because it's a practice with a canon: works people return to for centuries. The culture belongs to the form, not to any company that stages it. Netflix has been cultural before, but mostly as a verb, and verbs attach to practices that any platform can host. The streaming-era brand that came closest to becoming culture was HBO, and it did it with a canon. The Sopranos and The Wire are still referenced decades later. Netflix is often criticized for the opposite: a library of things watched once and forgotten.

That's the test I'd apply to Netflix now. Not subscriber counts or ad revenue, but whether it makes things people return to. If abundance pushes value toward what's live, shared and human, a company that only distributes content will keep losing evenings to the platform with the most of it. A company that becomes the place culture happens might keep the one thing AI can't make cheap.

Netflix beat the studios by making their strengths irrelevant. The question now is whether it can build the one strength abundance has never erased.
