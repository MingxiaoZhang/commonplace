Would It Still Be Me?

September 2026

It started, as these things do, with a half-remembered article about teleportation. The idea was that you could entangle a set of particles with every particle in a body, disassemble the person here, and reassemble them there. My first reaction was flat certainty: that kills them. Whoever steps out on the other end is a stranger with my memories. The real me ended on the pad.

What I didn't notice at first is how much that certainty was assuming. Why would it be death? The person who arrives has all my memories, intentions, personality — everything I'd point to if you asked me to describe myself. To insist they're not me, I have to believe there's some further thing, over and above all of that, which got left behind. Call it the further fact. The whole question turns on whether that thing exists.

Derek Parfit spent a career arguing it doesn't. A person, he says, isn't an extra ingredient sitting on top of a brain, a body, and a stream of mental events — the way a nation isn't an extra thing hovering above its people and land. Once you've fixed all the physical and psychological facts, there's nothing left over to settle. What connects me over time isn't an identity-essence; it's just psychological continuity — memories linking to experiences, intentions getting carried out, overlapping like fibers in a rope where no single strand runs the whole length. And his real bomb: that continuity is all we ever actually cared about. "Identity" was just riding along with it, and in the strange cases the two come apart.

His sharpest illustration is fission. Split a brain's two hemispheres into two bodies; both wake up with my memories. Which one is me? Both is impossible — I can't be two people. One but not the other is absurd — they're symmetric, nothing could make one me and the other a stranger. Neither is absurd too — surviving as a single hemisphere is ordinary (people live with half a brain removed), so how does doing it twice add up to dying? Every answer breaks. But there's no puzzle at all about the continuity: it simply holds, to both. So the thing that breaks is identity, and the thing that stays sensible is continuity. Identity, Parfit concludes, was never what mattered.

I wanted him to be right. If continuity is what matters and it doesn't need the original body, then death isn't necessarily the end of the story. But my gut kept siding with the other camp. The plain biological view says I simply am an organism, and a teleporter builds a new one from new atoms while destroying the old — no soul required, just biology. And underneath that sat a worry I couldn't shake: what I'm actually afraid of losing isn't my memories. It's the fact that there's someone home to have them. You could assemble a being with every one of my psychological contents and it would still be a different light switched on — a second experiencer, not the continuation of mine. Continuity only ever compares contents. It's blind, by construction, to whether the same someone is still there.

That worry has a natural home: death. If what I am is a particular experiencer rather than just a pattern, death is exactly the place where the pattern might be recoverable while the experiencer is not. So suppose some far-future technology could bring a dead person back. The idea immediately splits in two. One version scans the body, stores a description, and builds a fresh one to match — but that's just the teleporter again with a delay. Same copy, same problem; it could even build two. The other version doesn't rebuild from a record at all. It repairs the original: take the actual body, the same neurons, and physically reverse the damage that killed it. Now there's no description, no printout, and — this is the part that matters — no way to make two of anything. There's one body being restored. The thing that always bothered me about the teleporter was that a copy can be run twice, so no output has any better claim than another to be me. Repair the original and that whole objection dissolves.

I wanted to push harder, so I came at it from the opposite end: instead of fixing something already broken, start with an ordinary living person and change as little as physically possible. Freeze them. Cool a body until every neural process halts, hold it there, restart it later. This is the gentlest case of all — same matter, no copy, no way to fork, and the causal thread isn't even severed, just suspended. And it isn't pure fantasy at the edges. Surgeons already cool patients until the brain's activity goes flat, stop the heart, operate, and bring them back. Wood frogs freeze solid every winter and hop off in spring. Nobody thinks the frog that thaws is a replacement frog.

And this is where I hit the bottom. Because a frozen brain admits two descriptions. One: it's a paused person, and restarting it wakes the same subject. Two: it's a statue — a perfect three-dimensional record of a person, written in the original atoms — and restarting it boots up a new subject off that record. Here's what stopped me cold: those two descriptions are of the physically identical object. Same atoms, same positions, every measurement the same. Whether switching it back on re-lights me or lights someone new is settled by no physical fact whatsoever — because the two possibilities agree on everything anyone could ever observe.

That isn't a gap in the technology. I'd held the very atoms still and the question still wouldn't resolve. The leftover fact — same subject, or identical stranger? — is defined so that it makes no physical difference anywhere, ever. Which is precisely why no experiment can reach it. It was never an engineering problem disguised as a metaphysical one. It's a metaphysical one that no amount of engineering can touch.

And then the part that actually rearranged how I think about it: I can't verify this for someone waking from a coma either. Or for myself waking tomorrow. Or for myself one second from now. The teleporter never added the problem. The same undetectable gap sits inside the most ordinary night's sleep — "the same light stayed on" and "the old one went out, an identical one came on" make identical predictions about a perfectly normal Tuesday. The science fiction only dramatized something that was already there in the pillow.

The tidy way to sum all this up is that the continuous self is an illusion — that we're subtly different people from one moment to the next, and the feeling of sameness is just a story the brain tells convincingly. I reached for exactly that, and then backed away, because I don't think illusion is the right word. An illusion is a mismatch: the stick looks bent but is really straight. To call continuity an illusion, I'd need a real fact it's getting wrong — some truth that I am a different subject each moment, hiding behind the appearance of sameness. But that's the fact I'd just spent all night showing is undetectable, maybe empty. There's no straight stick behind the bent one. So the distortion isn't the continuity. It's the sharpness. My brain hands me a clean, all-or-nothing verdict — definitely me, definitely still here — over a reality that only ever offered matters of degree and no further fact. It imposes a crisp category where none exists.

I think that's an evolutionary artifact. A creature that feels like a solid, persisting self — one with a future worth defending — plans, fears its death, and out-survives one that doesn't. The self-model is tuned for staying alive, not for being accurate, and there's no reason those two should coincide. What's strange about being human is that the same brain eventually got good enough at reasoning to turn around and notice its own founding assumption is a fiction — while remaining completely unable to switch that fiction off. I can follow the entire argument and still flinch from an oncoming bus. Reasoning can see around the illusion; it can't disable it.

Which leaves death in an odd place. "Is death just like sleep?" is a question built by a brain that had to assume a sharp, permanent I to keep the body running in the first place. Turn that same brain's reasoning back on the assumption, and it finds no sharp I in there to be preserved or lost. The terror of death and the calm about sleep were both outputs of the same piece of machinery. Seeing that doesn't switch the terror off — the machinery keeps running regardless — but it does show the terror was never tracking the metaphysical fact I thought it was.

I fell asleep last night without a second thought about whether I'd be the same person in the morning. It turns out that's the whole answer that's available. The teleporter never deserved more fear than the pillow did — it was only ever borrowing it.
